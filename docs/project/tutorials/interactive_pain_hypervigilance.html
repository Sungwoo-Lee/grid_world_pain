0 Introduction & Roadmap

This interactive tutorial walks you through the computational models of pain, focusing on why the brain sometimes gets trapped in a state of chronic hypervigilance. By the end, you should be able to describe pain perception as precision-weighted inference, predict how reaction-time distributions shift with expectation, explain why guarding an injury can paradoxically prolong chronic pain, and unify all four frameworks under a single variational principle.

Why Does This Matter?

  • Clinical burden: Chronic pain affects ~20% of adults worldwide; it is the leading cause of disability globally (WHO).
  • Economic cost: Estimated > $600B/year in the U.S. alone (lost productivity + health-care), more than heart disease and cancer combined.
  • Scientific puzzle: Traditional biomedical models treat pain as tissue damage. But chronic pain often persists with no detectable pathology. The answer lies in how the brain computes pain — not in the tissue.
  • Therapeutic leverage: Understanding the computation of hypervigilance points directly to interventions: graded exposure, cognitive reappraisal, closed-loop neuromodulation, computational phenotyping of patients.

The Four Frameworks: A Nested Hierarchy

These are not four competing theories — they form a nested hierarchy, with the outermost containing the inner ones as special cases:

HV What is Hypervigilance?

"Hypervigilance" is in the title of this tutorial, so let's define it precisely before the computational models. The term has been used loosely in the literature, but the canonical definition is from Crombez, Van Damme & Eccleston (2005):

Definition (Crombez et al., 2005)

"Hypervigilance is an unintentional and efficient process that emerges when the threat value of pain is high, the fear system is activated, and the individual's current concern is to escape and avoid pain."

The key word is attentional. Hypervigilance is a cognitive-attentional phenomenon — not a sensory-receptor phenomenon.

The Four Attentional Manifestations

1. Attentional Capture

Pain-related cues (words, images, bodily sensations) automatically seize attention, disrupting ongoing tasks. The body-scan reflex.

2. Difficulty Disengaging

Once attention is captured by pain, it is hard to redirect. Rumination and catastrophizing emerge as the system gets stuck in pain-focused processing.

3. Interpretive Bias

Ambiguous stimuli are more likely to be classified as threatening or painful. A twinge becomes "damage"; a tingle becomes "spreading injury."

4. Body Scanning

Persistent, often unconscious monitoring of the body for threat signals. Drives anxiety, insomnia, and vigilance costs on other goals.

Important Distinction: Hypervigilance ≠ Sensitization

Hyperalgesia (amplified pain to noxious stimuli) and allodynia (pain from non-noxious stimuli) are sensitization phenomena — changes in peripheral or central nociceptive gain. They are not manifestations of hypervigilance per se.

However, the two are computationally related: sensitization increases the sensory precision \(\Pi_{\text{obs}}\) for nociceptive channels, which feeds into the attentional system and can promote hypervigilant scanning. The two processes co-occur clinically and reinforce each other, but they operate at different levels of the computational hierarchy. In this tutorial:

  • Sensitization appears in Section 3 as an inflated observation matrix \(C\).
  • Hypervigilance appears in Sections 1, 2, 4 as a specific configuration of precisions, boundaries, and prior beliefs.

Adaptive or Maladaptive?

Hypervigilance is not inherently a malfunction. From an evolutionary perspective, it is an adaptive strategy when the cost of underestimating injury (reopening a wound, infection, death) vastly exceeds the cost of overestimating it (losing a day of foraging). An organism that resumes activity too early is dead; one that rests an extra day is inconvenienced.

Cross-species evidence (~500 million years old): Cephalopods (octopus, cuttlefish, squid) — separated from mammals by half a billion years of evolution — show nearly identical post-injury behavior: nociceptor threshold reduction, wound-directed guarding for days, opioid modulation. This cross-phylum convergence reveals hypervigilance as an ancient, optimal-control solution to the universal computational problem of managing recovery under uncertainty.

The pathology: Hypervigilance becomes maladaptive when it fails to disengage after healing. Understanding why it gets stuck — and how to unstick it — is the central question this tutorial addresses computationally.

1 Bayesian Pain Inference

Pain is not a simple readout of nociceptive input — it is the posterior of a Bayesian inference combining top-down expectations (the prior) with bottom-up sensory evidence (the likelihood). The brain maintains a belief about the state of the body that is continuously updated by incoming sensory data. The posterior pain experience is a precision-weighted average: whichever source is more reliable (has lower variance, i.e., higher precision) dominates the percept.

This framework explains why the same physical stimulus can feel more or less painful depending on context: placebo analgesia, nocebo hyperalgesia, attentional modulation, and catastrophizing are all precision manipulations within this single equation.

Background & Theory — Why pain is not an alarm bell

The traditional "alarm bell" model treats pain as a straightforward readout of tissue damage: nociceptors detect a noxious stimulus, send a signal up the spinal cord, and the brain registers "ouch." Pain intensity should be proportional to stimulus intensity — more damage, more pain. This model is empirically wrong.

Evidence Against the Alarm Bell Model

  • Placebo analgesia: A sugar pill described as a painkiller genuinely reduces pain. No change in the stimulus, yet the experience changes.
  • Nocebo hyperalgesia: Warning someone that a procedure will be extremely painful makes the identical stimulus hurt more.
  • Distraction: Soldiers report not noticing severe battlefield injuries until combat ends.
  • Catastrophizing: Ruminating about pain amplifies the experience far beyond what nociceptive input would predict.
  • Chronic pain without tissue damage: Many chronic pain patients have no detectable peripheral pathology — the alarm is ringing, but there is no fire.
  • Phantom limb pain: The limb is gone, but the brain generates pain without any peripheral input.

These observations force a radical conclusion: pain is not a sensation — it is a perception constructed by the brain (Wiech, 2016). The nociceptive signal is just one input among many. The brain combines it with expectations, context, attention, memory, and goals to produce the experience we call pain.

Everyday Analogy: The Rustling Sound in the Forest

You are walking through a forest at dusk and hear a rustling sound. Is it the wind (harmless) or a snake (dangerous)? The sensory signal alone is ambiguous. Your brain must combine:

  • Prior beliefs: "This is a snake habitat; I just saw a warning sign"
  • Sensory evidence: The acoustic pattern of the rustle
  • Context: Dusk, low visibility, you're alone
  • Cost asymmetry: Missing a real snake is far worse than reacting to imaginary wind

Your final belief — and resulting behavior (freeze, step back, look carefully) — is not driven by the acoustic signal alone. It's a Bayesian posterior that weighs prior and evidence by their reliabilities. Pain works exactly the same way — but with nociceptive signals instead of acoustic ones.

Wiech (2016) frames this as "perception as inference" within a predictive coding framework: the brain generates predictions about expected sensory input, compares those predictions to actual nociceptive signals, and uses the discrepancy (prediction error) to update its beliefs. What you "feel" is not the raw signal — it is the brain's best estimate given everything it knows.

Worked Example: The Doctor's Warning

A patient is about to receive a heat stimulus on their arm. The brain has two sources of information:

  1. Prior expectation (\(\mu_{\text{prior}} = 7\)): Based on the doctor's warning, the patient expects a pain intensity of 7 out of 10.
  2. Sensory evidence (\(o = 4\)): The nociceptors report an actual intensity of 4 out of 10.

If pain were a simple readout, the patient would feel 4/10. But under Bayesian inference, the brain combines prior and evidence, weighted by their respective reliabilities (precisions). If the patient is very confident in the warning (high prior precision) and uncertain about the sensory signal, the experience might be 6/10 — pulled toward the expectation. If the patient trusts the sensory signal more, it might be 4.5/10.

Try it yourself: Use the sliders below to replicate this scenario. Set \(\mu_{\text{prior}} = 7\), \(\sigma_{\text{prior}} = 0.5\), \(o = 4\), and \(\sigma_{\text{obs}} = 2\). Watch how the green posterior is dragged toward the blue prior.

Neural substrates (Wiech, 2016): The likelihood \(P(o|x)\) is encoded in S1, S2, posterior insula, and lateral thalamus. The top-down prior is maintained by DLPFC and rostral ACC. The descending analgesia gate (ACC → PAG → spinal dorsal horn) modulates sensory precision. Prediction errors and valuation are processed in vmPFC and nucleus accumbens (mesolimbic circuit). A critical finding: cognitive self-regulation does not primarily modulate the Neurologic Pain Signature (S1/S2 pattern) — it acts through vmPFC–NAcc connectivity, changing the generative model rather than the early sensory encoding.

Prerequisite Concepts — Gaussian Distribution, Bayes' Theorem, Precision

The Gaussian (Normal) Distribution

A Gaussian distribution describes a bell-shaped curve centered on a mean \(\mu\) (the most likely value) with a standard deviation \(\sigma\) controlling the spread. Its probability density function is:

$$p(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

Variance \(\sigma^2\) measures how spread out the values are. A small \(\sigma\) means values cluster tightly around \(\mu\); a large \(\sigma\) means they are spread out. Precision is the inverse of variance: \(\Pi = 1/\sigma^2\). High precision = tight distribution = high confidence.

Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' theorem tells us how to update a belief when new evidence arrives:

$$P(\text{hypothesis} \mid \text{data}) = \frac{P(\text{data} \mid \text{hypothesis}) \cdot P(\text{hypothesis})}{P(\text{data})}$$

In words: the posterior (updated belief) is proportional to the likelihood (how well the data fits the hypothesis) times the prior (what we believed before seeing the data). The denominator \(P(\text{data})\) is just a normalizing constant that ensures probabilities sum to 1.

For pain: the hypothesis is "how much tissue damage is there?", the data is the nociceptive signal, the prior is the brain's expectation (shaped by context, learning, mood), and the posterior is the subjective pain experience.

Precision-Weighting: Why It Matters

When both prior and observation are Gaussian, the posterior mean is a weighted average where the weights are the precisions (inverse variances). This is not arbitrary — it is the mathematically optimal way to combine two uncertain sources of information. The more precise (reliable) source gets more weight.

This is why attention modulates pain: attending to pain increases the precision of nociceptive observations (\(\Pi_{\text{obs}} \uparrow\)), giving them more weight. Distraction does the opposite (\(\Pi_{\text{obs}} \downarrow\)), letting expectations dominate.

Why Precision (not variance)? — The Mathematical Motivation

Students often ask: why use precision \(\Pi = 1/\sigma^2\) rather than variance \(\sigma^2\) directly? The answer is elegant: when you multiply two Gaussians, their precisions add linearly:

$$\Pi_{\text{post}} = \Pi_{\text{prior}} + \Pi_{\text{obs}}$$

Precision is the natural quantity for Bayesian updating because combining sources of information is a sum-of-precisions operation. Variances don't combine this way (they require reciprocals). This is why the brain's neural circuitry for Bayesian inference uses precision-weighted prediction errors as its fundamental computational primitive (Friston, 2010).

Analogy: Think of precision as "votes." Each source of information casts votes proportional to its reliability. The posterior is the weighted average of their positions, where weights are the vote counts. More votes (higher precision) → more influence.

The posterior pain intensity \(\mu_{\text{post}}\), Kalman gain \(K\), and prediction error \(\delta\):

$$\mu_{\text{post}} = \frac{\Pi_{\text{obs}} \cdot o + \Pi_{\text{prior}} \cdot \mu_{\text{prior}}}{\Pi_{\text{obs}} + \Pi_{\text{prior}}}, \qquad K = \frac{\Pi_{\text{obs}}}{\Pi_{\text{obs}} + \Pi_{\text{prior}}}, \qquad \delta = o - \mu_{\text{prior}}$$

where \(\Pi = 1/\sigma^2\) is precision, \(o\) is the nociceptive observation, \(\mu_{\text{prior}}\) is the expected pain, \(K\) is how much the brain trusts new evidence, and \(\delta\) is the surprise.

Step-by-Step Derivation — From Bayes' theorem to the precision-weighted posterior

Step 1: Set up the generative model. Assume the brain models pain intensity \(x\) with a Gaussian prior and receives a noisy observation \(o\):

$$P(x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_{\text{prior}}, \sigma_{\text{prior}}^2), \qquad P(o \mid x) = \mathcal{N}(x, \sigma_{\text{obs}}^2)$$

Step 2: Apply Bayes' theorem. The posterior is proportional to the product of prior and likelihood:

$$P(x \mid o) \propto P(o \mid x) \cdot P(x) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{(o - x)^2}{2\sigma_{\text{obs}}^2}\right) \cdot \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - \mu_{\text{prior}})^2}{2\sigma_{\text{prior}}^2}\right)$$

Step 3: Combine the exponents. Adding the two quadratic terms in the exponent:

$$-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{(o-x)^2}{\sigma_{\text{obs}}^2} + \frac{(x-\mu_{\text{prior}})^2}{\sigma_{\text{prior}}^2}\right]$$

Step 4: Complete the square in \(x\). Expanding and collecting terms in \(x\), we get a new quadratic of the form \(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{\text{post}}^2}(x - \mu_{\text{post}})^2\), which is another Gaussian. The algebra yields:

$$\frac{1}{\sigma_{\text{post}}^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma_{\text{obs}}^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_{\text{prior}}^2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \Pi_{\text{post}} = \Pi_{\text{obs}} + \Pi_{\text{prior}}$$

The posterior precision is the sum of the two input precisions — combining information always reduces uncertainty.

Step 5: Find the posterior mean.

$$\mu_{\text{post}} = \frac{\Pi_{\text{obs}} \cdot o + \Pi_{\text{prior}} \cdot \mu_{\text{prior}}}{\Pi_{\text{obs}} + \Pi_{\text{prior}}}$$

This is a precision-weighted average. If \(\Pi_{\text{obs}} \gg \Pi_{\text{prior}}\), the posterior \(\approx o\) (trust the senses). If \(\Pi_{\text{prior}} \gg \Pi_{\text{obs}}\), the posterior \(\approx \mu_{\text{prior}}\) (trust the expectation).

Step 6: Rewrite as prediction error update (Kalman form).

$$\mu_{\text{post}} = \mu_{\text{prior}} + K \cdot \delta, \qquad K = \frac{\Pi_{\text{obs}}}{\Pi_{\text{obs}} + \Pi_{\text{prior}}}, \qquad \delta = o - \mu_{\text{prior}}$$

The brain starts with its expectation \(\mu_{\text{prior}}\), computes the prediction error \(\delta\) (how much the observation differed from expectation), and corrects by a fraction \(K\) (the Kalman gain). When \(K=1\), the brain fully trusts the new evidence; when \(K=0\), it ignores it entirely.

2 Drift-Diffusion Model of Pain Perception

Section 1 showed how the brain combines prior and evidence into a pain estimate. But this inference is not instantaneous — it unfolds over time. The Drift-Diffusion Model (DDM) captures this temporal process: a decision variable accumulates noisy sensory evidence moment by moment, drifting stochastically until it crosses a decision boundary. Crossing the upper boundary means "pain detected"; crossing the lower boundary means "no pain."

Pain expectations can bias this process in two ways: shifting the starting point (a decisional bias — the brain begins closer to "pain" before evidence arrives) or changing the drift rate (a sensory gain change — the evidence itself accumulates faster). Empirical studies show pain expectations primarily shift the starting point (Wiech, 2016).

Background & Theory — From evidence accumulation to pain decisions

Section 1 showed that pain is a Bayesian inference. But that picture is static — it computes a single posterior from a single observation. In reality, pain perception unfolds over time. Consider hearing a rustling sound in a forest: you don't instantly decide "snake" or "wind." You accumulate evidence moment by moment — each second of rustling provides another noisy sample — until you are confident enough to commit to a decision.

The mathematical framework for this temporal process is the Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT) (Wald, 1947). The brain keeps a running tally — a decision variable — that sums the evidence so far. Accumulation continues until the tally crosses a bound, triggering commitment to one choice. This produces the fundamental speed-accuracy tradeoff: high bounds require more evidence (slower, more accurate); low bounds require less (faster, more error-prone).

Neural Implementation: It's Not a Metaphor

Single-unit recordings in macaques performing the random-dot motion task revealed the neural implementation of the SPRT (Gold & Shadlen, 2007):

  1. Sensory neurons in area MT encode the momentary evidence — their firing rate reflects the direction and strength of the motion signal.
  2. Downstream neurons in area LIP accumulate this evidence over time — their firing rate ramps up like the decision variable.
  3. A stereotyped firing-rate threshold triggers the saccade — this is the neural bound.

The same logic applies to pain: a neural decision variable accumulates nociceptive evidence to a threshold. The Centro-Parietal Positivity (CPP), an EEG signal that builds to a fixed amplitude before each perceptual report, is the human correlate of this accumulator (O'Connell & Kelly, 2021).

Two Routes: Sensory Gain vs. Decisional Bias

A key empirical finding from pain psychophysics (Wiech, 2016): when participants are cued to expect high or low pain before receiving a stimulus, expectation predominantly shifts the starting point \(z\), not the drift rate \(v\). This means pain expectations act as a decisional bias (the brain begins closer to the "pain" decision before evidence arrives) rather than a sensory gain change (the brain doesn't literally accumulate evidence faster).

Drift rate changes were only observed when participants expected high pain but received low-intensity stimulation — i.e., when the expectation was maximally incongruent with the evidence.

Hypervigilance in DDM terms: (1) Starting point biased toward pain (\(z > a/2\)), (2) lowered boundary separation (\(a \downarrow\)), and possibly (3) elevated drift rate for nociceptive channels. Together, these produce faster pain detection, lower thresholds, more false alarms, and faster expectation-congruent errors. Under threat imminence, an urgency signal \(u(t)\) further collapses the boundary: \(a(t) = a_0 - u(t)\), shifting the system from deliberative to reflexive mode.

Prerequisite Concepts — Random Walks, Stochastic Processes, First-Passage Times

Random Walk & Brownian Motion

Imagine flipping a coin repeatedly: heads = step up, tails = step down. The running total forms a random walk. In continuous time and space, this becomes Brownian motion (or a Wiener process) \(W_t\) — a mathematical model of pure noise that fluctuates continuously.

Key property: \(W_t\) has independent, Gaussian increments. Over a small time interval \(dt\), the change is \(dW \sim \mathcal{N}(0, dt)\). This means increments scale as \(\sqrt{dt}\), not \(dt\) — a fundamental difference from deterministic dynamics.

Drift + Diffusion: The Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE)

The DDM adds a deterministic drift to the Brownian noise:

$$dZ = \underbrace{v\, dt}_{\text{drift (signal)}} + \underbrace{\sigma\, dW_t}_{\text{diffusion (noise)}}$$

If Itō notation looks scary, here is the equivalent discrete-time form that actually runs in the simulation below:

$$Z_{t+\Delta t} - Z_t = v\,\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\cdot\varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

At each small timestep \(\Delta t\), the decision variable moves by a deterministic nudge \(v\,\Delta t\) plus a random kick scaled by \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\). The square-root scaling is the crucial non-obvious feature of stochastic dynamics: noise accumulates slower than deterministic drift, which is why drift eventually wins with enough time.

Drift \(v\): the average direction of evidence accumulation. Positive \(v\) means evidence, on average, favors "pain." Larger \(|v|\) = stronger signal = easier decision.

Diffusion \(\sigma\): the noise amplitude. Larger \(\sigma\) = noisier evidence = more random paths = more errors.

Think of it as a biased coin-flip walk: the coin is slightly weighted (drift), but each flip is still random (diffusion).

Boundaries and First-Passage Time

The boundaries at \(Z=a\) (upper, "Pain") and \(Z=0\) (lower, "No Pain") are decision thresholds. The process terminates the first time \(Z(t)\) hits either boundary. The time at which this happens is the first-passage time — this is the reaction time (RT) in psychological terms.

Boundary separation \(a\) controls the speed-accuracy tradeoff: wide boundaries require more evidence (slow but accurate); narrow boundaries require less (fast but error-prone).

Starting point \(z\): where the accumulation begins. If \(z = a/2\), the process starts unbiased. If \(z > a/2\), it starts closer to the "Pain" boundary — a prior bias toward reporting pain.

The Drift-Diffusion equation with initial condition and boundaries:

$$dZ = v\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t, \quad Z(0) = z, \quad \text{boundaries at } 0 \text{ (No Pain) and } a \text{ (Pain)}$$

where \(v\) = drift rate (signal strength), \(\sigma\) = noise amplitude, \(z\) = starting point (prior bias), \(a\) = boundary separation (evidence threshold).

Step-by-Step Derivation — From SPRT to DDM, and connecting to Bayesian inference

Step 1: The Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT). Given a sequence of evidence samples \(e_1, e_2, \ldots\), we compute the log-likelihood ratio (evidence for "pain" vs "no pain"):

$$y_n = \sum_{i=1}^n \log\frac{P(e_i \mid \text{pain})}{P(e_i \mid \text{no pain})}$$

Each sample adds a weight \(w_i = \log[P(e_i|\text{pain})/P(e_i|\text{no pain})]\). The running sum \(y_n\) is the decision variable.

Step 2: Continuous-time limit. If evidence arrives continuously and each increment is Gaussian, the sum becomes a stochastic integral. The per-sample weight \(w_i\) has mean \(\mu\) (the true signal) and variance \(\sigma_w^2\) (the noise). In continuous time:

$$dy = \mu\, dt + \sigma_w\, dW_t$$

This is exactly the DDM equation, where drift \(v = \mu\) is the signal strength and diffusion \(\sigma = \sigma_w\) is the per-sample noise.

Step 3: Boundaries from error-rate control. SPRT stops when the cumulative evidence crosses a threshold. Wald (1947) showed the bounds are set by the desired error rates \(\alpha, \beta\):

$$\text{Upper bound: } A \approx \log\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha}, \qquad \text{Lower bound: } B \approx \log\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}$$

With symmetric error rates (\(\alpha = \beta\)), the bounds are symmetric: \(\pm \log\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\).

Step 4: Connection to Bayesian inference (Section 1). The starting point \(z\) encodes the prior:

$$z = \frac{a}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\log\frac{P(\text{pain})}{P(\text{no pain})}$$

If pain is expected (\(P(\text{pain}) > 0.5\)), \(z > a/2\) and the accumulator starts biased toward the "Pain" boundary. The drift rate \(v\) encodes the sensory evidence quality, mapping onto \(\Pi_{\text{obs}}\) in Section 1.

Key insight: The DDM is the continuous-time, Gaussian limit of the SPRT — the mathematically optimal sequential test. The brain physically implements it by accumulating neural firing-rate differences to a threshold.

3 LQG Control Loop — Injury Recovery

Sections 1-2 addressed perception: "how much does it hurt?" This section addresses control: "what should the organism do about it?" Post-injury pain is an optimal control problem. The body is a plant (biological tissue healing over time), nociceptors are sensors, and the brain is the controller that must infer the hidden injury state and select protective actions (rest, guard, analgesia).

The brain estimates the hidden injury state via a Kalman filter and issues commands that trade off the cost of being injured (\(Q\)) against the cost of protection (\(R\), e.g., missed foraging). When guarding restricts sensory feedback, the innovation signal vanishes and the estimated injury gets stuck at "injured" — even as the body heals. This is the information restriction hypothesis for chronic pain (Seymour et al., 2023).

Background & Theory — Three types of pain and why recovery is a control problem

Sections 1–2 addressed the question "how much does it hurt?" This section addresses a different question: "what should the organism do about it?" To understand why this requires control theory, we must first distinguish three fundamentally different types of pain (Seymour, Crook & Chen, 2023):

Three Types of Pain — Three Computational Problems

Acute nociception (milliseconds to seconds): A fast feedforward reflex to minimize ongoing damage. Touching a hot stove triggers withdrawal. This is the "alarm bell" — it works well and is computationally simple.

Post-injury pain (hours to weeks): Not signaling ongoing damage — it is managing recovery. The brain must infer a hidden variable (tissue healing state) from noisy signals and decide how much to protect vs. resume normal activity. This is an optimal control problem under uncertainty.

Chronic pain (months to years): What happens when the control system breaks down. The controller becomes stuck in "injured" mode, enforcing protection long after the tissue has healed.

The key insight of Seymour et al. (2023) is that post-injury pain is not an alarm — it is a recuperation controller. Its purpose is to regulate protective behavior during healing: suppressing movement, promoting rest, reallocating energy, and trading short-term utility (foraging, mating) for long-term tissue repair.

The Four Failure Modes of Chronic Pain

Seymour et al. (2023) describe four mechanisms that seed the transition from adaptive post-injury pain to maladaptive chronic pain:

  1. Maladaptive learning: Avoidance prevents the brain from discovering that actions no longer cause pain. Compounded by elevated punishment sensitivity.
  2. Maladaptive model: The brain's model doesn't account for central sensitization, so amplified nociceptive signals are interpreted as evidence of worse injury.
  3. Maladaptive integration: Nerve damage or amputation creates persistent sensory conflicts the brain cannot resolve.
  4. Maladaptive priors: Pessimistic beliefs ("it will never get better") override incoming recovery evidence.

These four modes feed each other in a positive-feedback trap — the formal origin of the acute-to-chronic transition. Use the "Chronic Pain" preset below to see all failure modes operating simultaneously.

Evolutionary Evidence: Hypervigilance Is 500 Million Years Old

Cephalopods (octopus, cuttlefish, squid) — separated from mammals by ~500 million years of evolution — show strikingly similar post-injury behavior: nociceptor threshold reduction, wound-directed guarding persisting for days, and opioid-modulated pain behavior. This cross-phylum convergence is strong evidence that post-injury pain is an ancient, optimal-control solution that has been independently reinvented because the underlying computational problem is universal.

Neuromodulatory implementation: Opioids modulate the Kalman gain \(L\) (descending analgesia reduces the innovation signal) and the observation matrix \(C\) (stress-induced analgesia suppresses nociceptor gain). Dopamine sets the effective \(Q/R\) ratio and controls action vigor. Post-injury downshift of mesolimbic dopamine raises the effective \(R\) on foraging — enforcing rest and low motivation (Gershman et al., 2024).

This protective action serves two functions: (1) it directly reduces the mechanical load on the tissue (accelerating healing via the control input \(u\)), and (2) critically, it drastically reduces the informativeness of sensory observations — the agent stops probing the limb, so the nociceptive channel is flooded with noise rather than information. This is the mechanism of information restriction.

Prerequisite Concepts — State-Space Models, Kalman Filter, Feedback Control

State-Space Models: Describing Dynamics

A state-space model describes how a system evolves over time using two equations:

$$x_{t+1} = A \cdot x_t + B \cdot u_t + w_t \qquad \text{(state equation: how injury evolves)}$$
$$y_t = C \cdot x_t + v_t \qquad \text{(observation equation: what the sensors see)}$$

\(x_t\) is the hidden state (true injury level — you can't directly observe inflammation inside your knee). \(y_t\) is the observation (nociceptive signal — what you feel). \(u_t\) is the control action (rest, guard, move). \(w_t\) and \(v_t\) are noise (biological variability and sensor unreliability).

\(A\) is the healing rate: if \(A < 1\), the injury naturally shrinks over time (spontaneous recovery). \(C\) is the sensor gain: larger \(C\) means the nociceptors amplify the injury signal (central sensitization).

The Kalman Filter: Optimal State Estimation

Since \(x_t\) is hidden, the brain must estimate it from noisy observations. The Kalman filter is the mathematically optimal estimator for linear-Gaussian systems. It works in two steps each timestep:

Predict: Use the model to forecast the next state: \(\hat{x}_{t|t-1} = A \hat{x}_{t-1} + B u_{t-1}\)

Update: Compare the prediction to the actual observation and correct:

$$\hat{x}_t = \hat{x}_{t|t-1} + L \cdot \underbrace{(y_t - C \hat{x}_{t|t-1})}_{\text{innovation (surprise)}}$$

The innovation is the prediction error — how much the observation surprised the estimator. The Kalman gain \(L\) controls how much of this surprise is absorbed into the estimate. This is the multivariate, temporal generalization of the Kalman gain \(K\) from Section 1.

Feedback Control: Closing the Loop

In feedback control, the controller observes the state (via the Kalman filter) and adjusts its actions to drive the state toward a goal. For injury recovery, the goal is \(x = 0\) (healed). The optimal control law is:

$$u_t^* = -K_{\text{control}} \cdot \hat{x}_t$$

When \(\hat{x}\) is large (believe the injury is severe), protect strongly. When \(\hat{x} \to 0\) (believe recovery is progressing), resume normal activity. The problem: if guarding suppresses the observations that would reveal recovery, \(\hat{x}\) never updates downward.

Plant dynamics, observation, and Kalman filter estimate:

$$\dot{x} = Ax + Bu + w, \quad y = Cx + v, \quad \dot{\hat{x}} = A\hat{x} + Bu + L(y - C\hat{x})$$

where \(x\) = true injury, \(\hat{x}\) = brain's estimate, \(y\) = nociceptive observation, \(u\) = control action (guarding), \(L\) = Kalman gain, \(A\) = healing rate, \(C\) = nociceptor sensitivity, \(w,v\) = noise.

Step-by-Step Derivation — Kalman gain and the information restriction mechanism

Step 1: The innovation and its role. The innovation is the difference between what the sensor reports and what the estimator predicted:

$$\text{innovation}_t = y_t - \hat{y}_t = y_t - C\hat{x}_t$$

When the innovation is zero, the observation contained no new information. The estimator learns nothing.

Step 2: The Kalman gain in 1D. The Kalman gain balances trust in the model vs. trust in the sensor:

$$L = \frac{P \cdot C}{C^2 \cdot P + V}$$

where \(P\) is the estimation error covariance (how uncertain the estimate is) and \(V\) is the observation noise. This is the same precision-weighting logic as Section 1: \(L\) increases when the sensor is reliable (\(V\) small) and decreases when it is noisy (\(V\) large).

Step 3: Information restriction mechanism. When the agent guards (avoids movement):

$$\text{Guarding ON} \Rightarrow V_{\text{eff}} \uparrow \uparrow \Rightarrow L \downarrow \Rightarrow \text{innovation absorbed} \downarrow \Rightarrow \hat{x} \text{ stuck}$$

Guarding makes observations uninformative (high effective noise \(V_{\text{eff}}\)). The Kalman gain drops, innovations have no effect, and the estimate \(\hat{x}\) stays at "injured" even as the true state \(x\) heals toward zero. The controller, seeing a high \(\hat{x}\), continues to guard. A self-reinforcing trap.

Step 4: Central sensitization inflates \(C\). If the nociceptor gain \(C\) increases without the brain knowing (no efference copy update), the brain treats amplified signals as evidence of greater injury:

$$C \uparrow \Rightarrow y = C \cdot x + v \text{ is larger} \Rightarrow \text{brain infers larger } \hat{x}$$

The innovation is inflated by the amplified \(C\), and the Kalman gain absorbs this as evidence of worsening injury — even if the tissue is actually healing.

4 Active Inference — The Chronic Pain Trap

Active inference is the most general framework in this tutorial — it subsumes Bayesian inference (Section 1), the DDM (Section 2), and LQG control (Section 3) as special cases. Its central claim: both perception and action serve a single objective — minimizing Expected Free Energy (EFE).

The EFE has two components: risk (avoiding outcomes the agent dislikes, e.g., pain) and ambiguity (seeking observations that reduce uncertainty). The agent selects the policy with the lowest total EFE. In chronic pain, risk dominates: the agent guards to avoid pain, but this starves the inference of corrective data, trapping beliefs in "injured" — the dark-room problem on interoception.

Background & Theory — The unifying framework and the dark-room problem

Active inference (Friston, 2010; Smith, Friston & Whyte, 2022) is the most general framework in this tutorial. Its central claim: both perception and action serve a single objective — minimizing surprise (equivalently, maximizing model evidence). An agent maintains a generative model of how hidden states in the world produce observations, and it acts to make its observations consistent with its model.

Active inference is not a competing framework — it contains the others as special cases:

  • Bayesian inference (Section 1) = perception as variational inference (minimizing free energy w.r.t. beliefs)
  • DDM (Section 2) = evidence accumulation is belief updating; boundary crossing is policy commitment
  • LQG control (Section 3) = the continuous, Gaussian, quadratic-cost special case of EFE minimization

The Exploration-Exploitation Dilemma in Injury Recovery

Consider an injured agent in the recovery phase. It faces a fundamental tension:

Exploitation (minimize risk): Select policies that minimize predicted nociceptive observations. These are protective policies — rest, guard, avoid movement. They are immediately effective: the agent avoids pain.

Exploration (minimize ambiguity): Select policies that provide informative observations about the healing state. These are active policies — test the injured limb, attempt movement, probe the boundary of pain. They increase short-term risk but decrease ambiguity.

The trap: Exploitation directly undermines exploration. Guarding suppresses movement → no movement means no sensory feedback → no feedback means no prediction errors to update the model → the model stays stuck on "injured" → the stuck model drives continued guarding. A self-reinforcing loop.

The Dark-Room Problem on Interoception

The classic "dark-room problem" asks: why doesn't an agent that minimizes surprise just sit in a dark room forever? For exteroceptive agents, the answer is that they have preferences for rich sensory experience (food, social interaction, etc.).

But for interoceptive pain channels, the preferences are monotonically negative — there is no nociceptive observation the agent actively seeks. The "dark room" (complete avoidance of all nociceptive input) is genuinely the EFE minimum under a stuck injury prior. This is why chronic pain is so resistant to resolution: the mathematics of the agent's own inference system conspire against recovery.

Catastrophizing = Excessive Prior Precision + Elevated Preference Precision

The catastrophizing literature maps directly onto active inference: catastrophizing is a state where (1) the agent is extremely confident it is injured (\(q(s=\text{injured})\) has very high precision), AND (2) even mild pain observations are perceived as catastrophically costly (\(C(\text{pain})\) is very strongly negative). This locks the agent into risk-minimization and prevents the epistemic actions that would allow recovery.

How active inference connects the frameworks: The value of active inference is that it provides a single, principled objective (minimize EFE) that naturally captures the tension between risk and ambiguity — the tension that is at the heart of chronic pain and hypervigilance. Safety (Section 6 of the tutorial) ≈ low risk in EFE; threat ≈ high risk; controllability ≈ availability of low-risk policies. The Kalman filter from Section 3 is the Gaussian variational posterior.

Prerequisite Concepts — KL Divergence, Entropy, Policies, POMDP

KL Divergence: Measuring the "Distance" Between Distributions

The Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence measures how different two probability distributions are:

$$D_{\text{KL}}[q \| p] = \sum_x q(x) \log\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}$$

It is always \(\geq 0\), and equals 0 only when \(q = p\) (the distributions are identical). Think of it as the "surprise cost" of using \(p\) when reality is \(q\). In active inference, risk = KL divergence between predicted observations and preferred observations.

Entropy: Measuring Uncertainty

Entropy \(H\) measures how uncertain or unpredictable a distribution is:

$$H[p(o|s)] = -\sum_o p(o|s) \log p(o|s)$$

High entropy = observations are uninformative (a coin flip gives maximum entropy). Low entropy = observations are informative (a loaded die is more predictable). In active inference, ambiguity = expected entropy of observations — how informative the policy's observations will be about the hidden state.

POMDP: Partially Observable Markov Decision Process

The world has hidden states \(s\) (injured vs. healed) that the agent cannot directly observe. Instead, it receives observations \(o\) (pain vs. no pain) that are probabilistically related to the state. The agent maintains a belief \(q(s)\) about the state and selects policies \(\pi\) (sequences of actions) to achieve its goals.

In our pain model: states = {injured, healed}, observations = {pain, no_pain}, policies = {Explore (test the limb), Guard (rest/avoid), Mixed}. The key question: does the agent explore to learn whether it has healed, or guard to avoid potential pain?

Preferences: What the Agent Wants

Unlike classical RL (which uses reward functions), active inference encodes goals as preferred observations \(p(o|C)\). The agent prefers observations consistent with a healthy body:

$$p(o|C) \propto \exp(C(o))$$

With \(C(\text{no pain}) \gg C(\text{pain})\). The preference precision controls how strongly the agent is averse to pain observations. Higher precision = more aversive = stronger drive to avoid pain at all costs.

Expected Free Energy (EFE) decomposes into risk and ambiguity:

$$G_\pi = \underbrace{D_{\text{KL}}\bigl[q(o|\pi) \| p(o|C)\bigr]}_{\text{risk: avoid unpreferred outcomes}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q(s|\pi)}\!\left[H[p(o|s)]\right]}_{\text{ambiguity: seek information}}$$

The agent selects the policy \(\pi^* = \arg\min_\pi G_\pi\). Risk drives exploitation (avoid pain); ambiguity drives exploration (gain information). The chronic pain trap occurs when risk overwhelms ambiguity.

Step-by-Step Derivation — EFE decomposition and why guarding creates a trap

Step 1: Expected observations under a policy. Given the agent's belief \(q(s)\) about the hidden state and the policy's observation model \(p(o|s)\), the predicted observations are:

$$q(o|\pi) = \sum_s p(o|s,\pi) \cdot q(s)$$

If the agent believes it is probably injured (\(q(\text{injured}) = 0.8\)) and exploring produces pain when injured, then \(q(\text{pain}|\text{explore})\) is high.

Step 2: Risk term. Risk measures how far the predicted observations are from what the agent wants:

$$\text{Risk} = D_{\text{KL}}[q(o|\pi) \| p(o|C)] = \sum_o q(o|\pi) \log\frac{q(o|\pi)}{p(o|C)}$$

If the agent predicts it will observe pain but prefers not to, risk is high. Guarding reduces risk because its uninformative observations are less likely to be painful.

Step 3: Ambiguity term. Ambiguity measures how informative the policy's observations will be:

$$\text{Ambiguity} = \sum_s q(s) \cdot H[p(o|s,\pi)]$$

Explore has low ambiguity: its observations reliably distinguish injured from healed (low entropy \(H\)). Guard has high ambiguity: its observations are uninformative (high entropy — you learn nothing by staying still).

Step 4: The trap. For a hypervigilant agent with high \(q(\text{injured})\) and high preference precision:

$$G_{\text{explore}} = \underbrace{\text{HIGH}}_{\text{predicts pain obs}} + \underbrace{\text{low}}_{\text{informative}}$$
$$G_{\text{guard}} = \underbrace{\text{low}}_{\text{avoids pain obs}} + \underbrace{\text{HIGH}}_{\text{uninformative}}$$

If preference precision is high enough, the risk term for "explore" overwhelms its ambiguity advantage. The agent selects "guard" — and never obtains the data that would correct its belief. After the tissue heals at step 12, the exploring agent detects this (beliefs update down); the guarding agent does not (beliefs stay stuck). This is the chronic pain trap.

EFE in Plain English

$$G_\pi = \underbrace{D_{\text{KL}}\bigl[q(o|\pi) \| p(o|C)\bigr]}_{\text{RISK}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q(s|\pi)}\!\left[H[p(o|s)]\right]}_{\text{AMBIGUITY}}$$

RISK (first term): "How much do the outcomes I expect from this policy differ from what I want?" If this policy predicts lots of pain and I hate pain, risk is high. Minimizing risk drives exploitation — choose policies that yield preferred outcomes.

AMBIGUITY (second term): "How uncertain am I about what I'll actually see if I follow this policy?" If the policy's observations don't reliably distinguish "injured" from "healed," ambiguity is high. Minimizing ambiguity drives exploration — choose policies that provide information.

The total EFE is their sum. Lower is better. The agent selects \(\pi^* = \arg\min_\pi G_\pi\).

Check Your Understanding

Predict: Under the "Chronic Pain Trap" preset, can the agent be unstuck just by lowering preference precision (making pain less aversive)? Or does the prior on injury also need to change?

Show answer
Lowering preference precision alone is partially sufficient but often insufficient. As you lower pref precision, risk shrinks and "Explore" becomes competitive. But if the prior is locked at \(P(\text{injured}) = 0.95\), the agent still starts from a deeply-held injury belief. Graded exposure therapy therefore does both: (1) lowers the pain-aversiveness via safety learning (pref precision ↓), and (2) generates prediction errors that gradually update the prior (\(P(\text{injured})\) ↓). Try it: set Chronic Pain Trap, then manually drag pref precision to 0.5 while keeping prior at 0.95 — watch Explore become selected but beliefs still drift slowly.

How Graded Exposure Therapy Breaks the Trap

Graded exposure therapy (the gold-standard treatment for chronic pain) works exactly by forcing the agent out of "Guard" and into "Explore":

  • Small, safe movements give the agent a low-risk way to test the injured region. Each small movement generates a prediction error; accumulated errors gradually update the prior on injury.
  • Graded difficulty (hierarchy of exposures) ensures the agent never faces a movement whose predicted pain would dominate. The risk term stays low enough that the ambiguity term can meaningfully drive policy selection.
  • Safety learning lowers the preference precision for pain observations (the nervous system relaxes its catastrophizing interpretation). Both levers are pulled at once.

In computational terms: exposure therapy is a clinically-engineered way of forcing Explore policies, thereby restoring the epistemic action that the chronic pain trap had collapsed.

★ Synthesis: Hypervigilance Across the Four Models

Having walked through four computational frameworks, we can now assemble a unified characterization of hypervigilance. Although clinicians observe a single phenomenon, computationally it is a coherent pattern of parameter deviations that spans every level of the decision-making hierarchy.

The Unifying Theme: Precision Dysregulation

Across all four frameworks, hypervigilance reduces to a single computational motif: precision dysregulation — the mis-calibration of confidence weights in the brain's inference and control machinery.

  • Bayesian: precision is explicit (\(\Pi_{\text{prior}}, \Pi_{\text{obs}}\))
  • DDM: precision maps onto starting point and boundary
  • LQG: precision is the Kalman gain \(L\) and observation matrix \(C\)
  • Active inference: precision is the risk–ambiguity balance in EFE

These frameworks are mathematically nested: Active Inference ⊃ LQG ⊃ Bayesian ≡ DDM (limiting case). Precision dysregulation at the active-inference level propagates down through all the special cases, producing the full clinical syndrome.

From Theory to Clinical Practice

How do we unstick the chronic pain trap? The computational framework points directly to interventions:

  • Graded exposure therapy: Forces the agent into "Explore" policies, generating new prediction errors that can update a stuck prior (Viz 4).
  • Cognitive reappraisal: Directly manipulates the prior \(\mu_{\text{prior}}\) by changing the generative model's context (Viz 1).
  • Mindfulness / attention training: Reconfigures precision weights \(\Pi_{\text{obs}}\) to prevent attentional capture by pain (Viz 1, Viz 4).
  • Closed-loop neuromodulation (e.g., SCS/DBS): Uses real-time state estimation (\(\hat{x}\)) and Model Predictive Control to directly modulate the LQG loop (Viz 3).
  • Computational phenotyping: Instead of vague diagnostic labels, characterize each patient by their parameter vector (Mahajan & Seymour 2025). Enables personalized therapy selection.

Forward Pointer: The Grid-World Pain Project

This tutorial is the theoretical foundation for the Interoceptive AI — Grid-World Pain research project. The goal of that project is to implement the frameworks you've just explored in a reinforcement-learning agent that must balance exteroceptive goals (foraging for food) with interoceptive homeostasis (avoiding injury, recovering from damage).

The agent architecture instantiates:

  • Bayesian precision as a state-dependent observation noise (injury modulates sensor reliability)
  • LQG control as a homeostatic reward function \(r = \Delta \text{drive}\) where drive combines satiation and injury
