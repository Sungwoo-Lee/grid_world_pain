Grid-world agent · 1,000,000 episodes · 189,906,610 steps

What makes this agent hide?

A trained forager was returned to the world it grew up in and lived a million separate lives. The record of those lives says it hides from a smell it cannot interpret — and that the mistake is measurably fatal.

Our agent is a small creature in a ten-by-ten field. It must eat to stay alive, and it must avoid predators that hunt it. Bushes scattered around the field will hide it — but a bush has no food in it, so every moment spent hidden is a moment not spent eating. That is the whole of its problem.

We trained it, froze it, and then put it back into exactly the world it had learned in, for a million episodes. At the start of each episode the world rerolls itself: how many predators appear, how far each can see, how many bushes exist, how much food, and even how injured and how hungry the agent begins. Those dice are thrown before the agent does anything, which is what makes this useful — it is a randomised experiment the world runs on itself, a million times, so we can read off causes rather than mere correlations.

First, what the creature can actually sense

Every result below turns on this, so it comes first. Reading the settings the run itself saved:

sight
Range zero. It sees only the tile it is standing on. At any distance it is blind.
smell
Its only distant sense, reaching across the whole field.
own injury
Not perceivable. It has no channel telling it how wounded it is.
pain
Perceivable — but only as a fading trace of being struck, not as a standing wound.
hunger
Perceivable.

And here is the trap that was built into this world deliberately. There are two kinds of animal: predators, which kill, and rabbits, which are entirely harmless. They look different. But their scents overlap heavily — a predator's odour is drawn around one point, a rabbit's around another, and the spread of each is wide enough that they routinely trade places. Since the agent is blind at distance, scent is all it has to go on.

The headline

Because scent is redrawn at random for every animal in every episode, we can watch what happens when a predator happens to smell strongly of predator — and, separately, when a harmless rabbit happens to smell exactly the same way.

The agent is not being stupid. It is doing the only thing available to a blind animal with an ambiguous nose: treat a predator-ish smell as a predator. The world simply does not let it find out which is which until too late.

Hiding does buy real protection — death by predator falls in both cases. But when the threat was never there, the protection is worth nothing and the missed meals are real. The agent starves instead.

Is it really mistaking the rabbit — or just jumpy in general?

A fair objection: perhaps a predator-ish smell anywhere in the field simply makes the agent nervous, and it hides more everywhere. That would be a mood, not a mistake. The two can be told apart, because a mistake should be aimed — the extra hiding ought to appear specifically in the moments when the misleading rabbit is close by.

When that rabbit comes near, hiding jumps from 26.7% to 49.8% — close to how this agent treats a genuine predator, 56.4%. It is not in a general mood. It has mistaken a specific harmless animal for a specific dangerous one.

Everything that moves the behaviour, ranked

Every randomly-set feature of the world, ordered by how strongly it shifts hiding. The bar length is percentage points of the episode spent hidden, per one standard deviation of that feature — a common scale, so that "number of predators" and "how far a predator can see" can honestly be compared.

Three more things the million episodes settle

Nearness is the whole story, moment to moment

To rule out the obvious objection — that a predator lingers because the agent is sitting in a bush — we used each animal's position on the previous step to predict hiding on the current one. The effect gets stronger, not weaker, which is the wrong direction for that objection — though consecutive steps resemble one another, so this weakens the objection rather than killing it outright.

Being injured does not make it hide

This one is worth care, because the raw numbers appear to say the opposite. Steps taken at severe injury show 26.3% hiding against 17.8% at no injury — which reads like a wounded animal turning cautious.

It isn't. The world hands the agent a random injury level, from none to nearly fatal, at the start of every episode. If injury caused caution, that is exactly where it would appear. Instead hiding drifts gently down, from 17.7% to 15.4%, as the starting wound gets worse.

Wounds heal quickly here — a randomly assigned one is gone within about twenty steps — so the fair place to look is inside that window, while the injury is still real. Over the first ten steps the badly-wounded group carries five times the injury of the lightly-wounded group, and hides one percentage point less:

The raw gradient was a confound. Split it by what was actually happening at the time and it falls apart — the sign does not even hold:

Injury was standing in for "a predator was around" — and that is the thing doing the work.

Hunger looks like a cause and is actually a footprint

The raw numbers again look emphatic: a starving agent hides on 50.2% of its steps, a well-fed one on 9.1%. Read straight, hunger drives hiding. It runs the other way.

7.3%of steps inside a bush involve eating
25.7%of steps outside a bush do

And when we set the agent's hunger at random and watch the first ten steps, it moves the opposite way from the raw numbers — agents that start starving hide less, not more. They leave cover to go and find food, which is exactly what a sensible animal should do.

So low nutrition is mostly the mark hiding leaves behind, not its cause. Two things produce that mark: hiding blocks eating, and a nearby predator both pins the agent in a bush and keeps it off the food. This design cannot say how the blame divides between them — but it can say the arrow does not run from hunger to hiding.

How much to trust this

causal
Anything the world rolled before the agent moved — predator count, detection range, scents, bush and food counts, starting injury and hunger.
correlational
Anything produced during the episode — nearness, injury sustained, nutrition spent, damage taken. These are partly the agent's own doing.
p-values
Useless here. With a million episodes everything is "significant". Everything above is ranked by size of effect, never by significance.
truncation
A factor that shortens episodes could inflate its own apparent effect, since late steps carry less hiding. Recomputed on a fixed early window, every effect gets larger — so truncation is not generating them.
scope
One trained agent, one run. Nothing here compares training conditions.
Outcome is the share of the agent's own chosen steps spent inside a bush; the spawn step is excluded, since the agent did not choose where it woke.
