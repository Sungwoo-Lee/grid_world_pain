Grid-world agent · 1,000,000 episodes · 189,906,610 steps

What makes this agent hide?

A trained forager was returned to the world it grew up in and lived a million separate lives. The record of those lives says it hides from a smell it cannot interpret, and that the mistake is measurably fatal. What its pain contributes, and whether a neuromodulator fixes any of it, turn out to be harder questions than they look.

Our agent is a small creature in a ten-by-ten field. It must eat to stay alive, and it must avoid predators that hunt it. Bushes scattered around the field will hide it — but a bush has no food in it, so every moment spent hidden is a moment not spent eating. That is the whole of its problem.

We trained it, froze it, and then put it back into exactly the world it had learned in, for a million episodes. At the start of each episode the world rerolls itself: how many predators appear, how far each can see, how many bushes exist, how much food, and even how injured and how hungry the agent begins. Those dice are thrown before the agent does anything, which is what makes this useful — it is a randomised experiment the world runs on itself, a million times, so we can read off causes rather than mere correlations.

First, what the creature can actually sense

Every result below turns on this, so it comes first. Reading the settings the run itself saved:

Range zero. It sees only the tile it is standing on. At any distance it is blind.

Perceivable, but blurred and delayed. It feels a smeared echo of how wounded it has been over the last dozen steps — never the wound it has right now.

Perceivable.

And here is the trap that was built into this world deliberately. There are two kinds of animal: predators, which kill, and rabbits, which are entirely harmless. They look different. But their scents overlap heavily — a predator's odour is drawn around one point, a rabbit's around another, and the spread of each is wide enough that they routinely trade places. Since the agent is blind at distance, scent is all it has to go on.

The headline

Because scent is redrawn at random for every animal in every episode, we can watch what happens when a predator happens to smell strongly of predator — and, separately, when a harmless rabbit happens to smell exactly the same way.

The agent is not being stupid. It is doing the only thing available to a blind animal with an ambiguous nose: treat a predator-ish smell as a predator. The world simply does not let it find out which is which until too late.

Hiding does buy real protection — death by predator falls in both cases. But when the threat was never there, the protection is worth nothing and the missed meals are real. The agent starves instead.

Is it really mistaking the rabbit — or just jumpy in general?

A fair objection: perhaps a predator-ish smell anywhere in the field simply makes the agent nervous, and it hides more everywhere. That would be a mood, not a mistake. The two can be told apart, because a mistake should be aimed — the extra hiding ought to appear specifically in the moments when the misleading rabbit is close by.

When that rabbit comes near, hiding jumps from 26.7% to 49.8% — close to how this agent treats a genuine predator, 56.4%. It is not in a general mood. It has mistaken a specific harmless animal for a specific dangerous one.

Everything that moves the behaviour, ranked

Every randomly-set feature of the world, ordered by how strongly it shifts hiding. The bar length is percentage points of the episode spent hidden, per one standard deviation of that feature — a common scale, so that "number of predators" and "how far a predator can see" can honestly be compared.

Three more things the million episodes settle

Nearness is the whole story, moment to moment

To rule out the obvious objection — that a predator lingers because the agent is sitting in a bush — we used each animal's position on the previous step to predict hiding on the current one. The effect gets stronger, not weaker, which is the wrong direction for that objection — though consecutive steps resemble one another, so this weakens the objection rather than killing it outright.

Pain: a blurred echo, and what we can honestly say about it

The agent does feel its wounds — but through a strange window. Each step it receives a blurred average of how hurt it has been over the last dozen steps, weighted towards about three steps ago, never including the present moment. A fresh wound is not yet felt; a healed one still is.

This part of the analysis was rebuilt after review found a bug in it, and the corrected story is more modest than the first version. It is worth reading for what it rules out as much as for what it shows.

Cover genuinely works

Across 10,779,288 moments when the agent took damage, it was inside a bush on exactly zero of them. Not "almost never" — zero. Predators cannot enter bushes, and the two other things that can hurt the agent must be stood on. This is a fact about how the world is built, and it also confirms our hiding measure means what its name says.

It explains something in the next chart too: the agent's exposure rises in the moments before it gets hit, because being out in the open is precisely how it got hit.

Getting hurt produces a reaction — a modest one

An earlier version of this page claimed eight points, sustained for twenty-five steps. That was measured from the wrong baseline and on the flattering subset. It has been corrected.

The same pain means different things

This is the sturdiest result here. With no predator anywhere near, high pain accompanies nearly three times the hiding of low pain — 11.5% up to 32.7%. With a predator right there, the agent is already hiding most of the time and pain adds proportionally little. Pain seems to matter most when there is nothing else to go on.

The same pain, but where did it come from?

Here is the sharpest thing the data say about pain. Take only the moments when the creature feels a lot of pain and no predator is anywhere near, and split them by how it came to be hurt.

An identical feeling, a near-doubled response, depending only on whether something actually attacked. That is what you would expect if the creature treats pain as news that a predator is about rather than as something to flinch from — and in this world that inference is correct, because being attacked is the only way to get hurt.

The one case where the inference fails is precisely the wound it woke up with. And there, the response is largely absent.

What we cannot say

Hunger looks like a cause and is actually a footprint

The raw numbers again look emphatic: a starving agent hides on 50.2% of its steps, a well-fed one on 9.1%. Read straight, hunger drives hiding. It runs the other way.

7.3%of steps inside a bush involve eating
25.7%of steps outside a bush do

And when we set the agent's hunger at random and watch the first ten steps, it moves the opposite way from the raw numbers — agents that start starving hide less, not more. They leave cover to go and find food, which is exactly what a sensible animal should do.

So low nutrition is mostly the mark hiding leaves behind, not its cause. Two things produce that mark: hiding blocks eating, and a nearby predator both pins the agent in a bush and keeps it off the food. This design cannot say how the blame divides between them — but it can say the arrow does not run from hunger to hiding.

Does it matter which of the ten agents you use?

We trained ten of these creatures, identical except for one dial: how much extra healing they earn for continuing to rest. Then we dropped all ten through the same million worlds — the same predators, the same bushes, the same starting wounds. So any difference between them is the creature, never the world.

Both the creature and the world are fully deterministic, so this is not a sample — it is the complete table. Which means the variation can be split up exactly.

So the ten are near-identical on average and quite different case by case. Averaged over a million worlds they all hide about a quarter of the time; put any two in the same world and they often do different things.

Does the healing dial explain it? We can't say.

Survival does fall steadily as the healing bonus rises — the ten values line up almost perfectly in order. It looks like a clean dose-response. Four checks say we cannot claim it:

ruled out
They all trained for exactly the same length — 100 million steps, to within 79. Not that.
mechanism
The obvious story is "bigger bonus → rests more → forages less → starves". Eating does fall. But resting shows no trend at all. The story's first step doesn't happen.
one run each
Every arm trained from the same seed, one run per dial setting. A shared seed fixes where they start, not where they end up — change the dial and the training paths diverge immediately. Each setting is a single roll of the dice.
machines
The runs were handed to compute nodes in dial order. So "which machine" tracks "which dial setting" almost perfectly — and predicts survival slightly better than the dial does. There is no obvious reason a machine should matter, and far too few of them to test. But the design cannot tell them apart.

What would fix it: repeat seeds rather than adding dial settings. Three repeats at four settings beats one repeat at ten, because it measures how much two identical runs differ — which is the number you need to judge whether any of this is real. And hand runs to machines in random order.

Does giving it a neuromodulator help?

A separate line of work adds a small extra network — a modulator — that reads the creature's internal state and turns the volume up or down on what it perceives. The hope is straightforward: a creature that can adjust its own senses by context ought to get better at telling a real threat from a false one. Given what we just saw about the rabbit, that would be worth a great deal.

The archive contains pairs of trained creatures that let us test this cleanly. Within a pair they are identical in every respect — same world, same body, same senses, same training length, same random seed, even the same moment in training — except that one has the modulator and one does not. We ran each pair through the same million worlds, and we did it for two separate pairs.

The first pair said the modulator makes things worse. The second pair, raised the same way, said it makes things dramatically better — lifting the creature above the line for the only time anywhere in this study.

Both cannot be true of the modulator. What this actually shows is that a single pair of creatures cannot answer the question at all. Raise one modulated creature and one plain one and the difference between them is as much about how those two individuals happened to grow up as about the modulator. We only found this out by doing it twice — and the honest conclusion is that whether the modulator helps perception is still unknown.

One thing both pairs agree on: the plain creature always sits below the line. Left to itself, it reacts more to the harmless rabbit than to the real predator, in both worlds we looked at.

It does live a little longer — but barely, and only when hunted

Here the two pairs do agree. The modulated creature survives a little longer in both — 2.8 steps in the first pair, 1.9 in the second. That sounds like something until you look at where it comes from:

30.7%of worlds where it does better
40.9%where the two are identical
28.4%where it does worse

One thing about it is genuinely encouraging, though. The benefit is entirely conditional on being hunted: with no predator in the world the modulator is very slightly harmful (−0.3 steps), with one predator it is worth +6.2, with two, +2.5. Whatever it is doing, it does it when there is a threat to respond to — which is at least the right shape for the idea, even if the size disappoints.

And both pairs agree on one more thing, which may be the most solid result here: the modulated creature eats more, starves less, and gets caught more. It trades safety for food. In the second pair the trade is sizeable — three and a half points of starvation converted into nearly four points of being caught.

What to take from this. The modulator reliably changes how the creature makes its living, and reliably makes almost no difference on average while changing a great deal case by case. Whether it improves perception is still an open question, and answering it needs more than two pairs.

How much to trust this

causal
Anything the world rolled before the agent moved — predator count, detection range, scents, bush and food counts, starting injury and hunger.
correlational
Anything produced during the episode — nearness, injury sustained, nutrition spent, damage taken. These are partly the agent's own doing.
p-values
Useless here. With a million episodes everything is "significant". Everything above is ranked by size of effect, never by significance.
truncation
A factor that shortens episodes could inflate its own apparent effect, since late steps carry less hiding. Recomputed on a fixed early window, every effect gets larger — so truncation is not generating them.
scope
One trained agent, one run. Nothing here compares training conditions.
Outcome is the share of the agent's own chosen steps spent inside a bush; the spawn step is excluded, since the agent did not choose where it woke.
