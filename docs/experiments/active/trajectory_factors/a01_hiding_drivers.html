What makes this agent hide?

We put trained agents back into the worlds they grew up in and watched them live millions of lives. This is the full record: what drives them into cover, what their pain does and does not explain, and whether a neuromodulator changes any of it.

Our agent is a small creature in a ten-by-ten field. It must eat to stay alive and avoid predators that hunt it. Bushes scattered around the field will conceal it — but a bush holds no food, so every moment hidden is a moment not spent eating. That trade is the whole of its problem, and "how much does it hide?" is the single number this document is about.

The method throughout is the same. Take a trained agent, freeze it, and drop it back into exactly the world it learned in for a million episodes. At the start of each episode the world rerolls itself: how many predators appear, how far each can see, how many bushes and how much food exist, and even how injured and how hungry the agent begins. Those dice are thrown before the agent does anything, which is what makes the exercise useful — it is a randomised experiment the world runs on itself, a million times over, so we can read off causes rather than mere correlations.

Two words are used precisely throughout. Caused means the world rolled it before the agent could influence it — those we can trust. Associated means it happened during the episode and is partly the agent's own doing — those we can only describe. Every claim below is labelled as one or the other.

1  What the creature can actually sense

Every result turns on this, so it comes first. These are read from the settings each run itself saved, not from what we assumed.

And here is a trap built into the world on purpose. There are two kinds of animal: predators, which kill, and rabbits, which are entirely harmless. They look different — but their smells overlap heavily. A predator's odour is drawn around one point, a rabbit's around another, and the spread of each is wide enough that they routinely trade places. Since the creature is blind at distance, smell is all it has.

3  The mistake it makes

Because every animal's smell is redrawn at random each episode, we can watch what happens when a predator happens to smell strongly of predator — and, separately, when a harmless rabbit happens to smell exactly the same way.

The creature is not being stupid. It is doing the only thing available to a blind animal with an ambiguous nose: treat a predator-ish smell as a predator. The world simply does not let it find out which is which until too late.

Is it really mistaking the rabbit, or just generally jumpy?

A fair objection: perhaps a predator-ish smell anywhere simply makes the creature nervous, so it hides more everywhere. That would be a mood, not a mistake. The two can be told apart, because a mistake should be aimed.

5  Pain

The creature feels its wounds, but through a strange window: a blurred average of how hurt it has been over the last dozen steps, weighted towards about three steps ago, never including right now. A fresh wound is not yet felt; a healed one still is. That blur is useful to us, because it means the feeling and the wound routinely disagree.

The same pain, but where did it come from?

This is the sharpest thing the data say about pain. Take the moments when the creature feels pain and no predator is near, and split them by how it came to be hurt.

That is what you would expect if the creature treats pain as news that a predator is about rather than as something to flinch from. In this world that inference is sound: being attacked is the only way to get hurt. The one case where it fails is precisely the wound it woke up with.

Note the other direction in that figure too: beyond one strike, more strikes means less hiding. A creature struck five times is one that kept failing to reach cover, so the strike count marks a hopeless situation as much as a memory.

6  Hunger

25.7%of steps outside a bush do

So low nutrition is mostly the mark hiding leaves behind, not its cause. Two things produce that mark — hiding blocks eating, and a nearby predator both pins the creature in a bush and keeps it off the food. This design cannot say how the blame divides, but it can say the arrow does not run from hunger to hiding.

7  Does it matter which trained creature you use?

We trained ten, identical except for one dial — how much extra healing they earn for continuing to rest — and dropped all ten through the same million worlds. Any difference between them is the creature, never the world.

Why we can't credit the healing dial

ruled out
All ten trained for the same length — 100 million steps, to within 79. Not that.
mechanism
The obvious story is "bigger bonus → rests more → forages less → starves". Eating does fall. But resting shows no trend at all. The story's first step never happens.
one run each
Every creature came from the same starting seed, one run per dial setting. A shared seed fixes where they start, not where they end up.
machines
Runs were handed to computers in dial order, so "which machine" tracks "which setting" almost perfectly — and predicts survival slightly better than the dial does.

8  Does a neuromodulator help?

A separate line of work adds a small extra network — a modulator — that reads the creature's internal state and turns the volume up or down on what it perceives. The hope is specific: a creature that can adjust its senses according to how hurt or hungry it is should respond to the world more appropriately for its situation.

The archive holds four pairs of trained creatures that test this cleanly. Within each pair they are identical in every respect — same world, same body, same senses, same training length, same starting seed, the same moment in training — except one has the modulator and one does not. We ran all four pairs through the same million worlds each.

The split is not random, though. Both pairs where the modulator helped came from a world where predators must touch the creature to hurt it; both where it hurt came from a world where predators can pounce from two or three tiles away. And the survival effect splits along a different line entirely — by how the training algorithm estimates future reward. With only two pairs on each side, that could still be coincidence, but it is the pattern to watch.

9  The measure built for the modulator

The comparisons above ask whether the modulated creature behaves differently. That is the wrong question for what a modulator is meant to do. A modulator adjusts perception according to internal state — so the sharp prediction is that its reaction to a nearby predator should depend more on how hurt it is than an unmodulated creature's does.

We measure that directly. For each level of felt pain, we compute how much more the creature hides when a predator is near than when none is — call that its alarm response at that pain level. Then we ask how much that response changes across pain levels.

There are two versions of this and the difference matters. In the observed version the creature's pain is its own doing, so the pattern describes its situation. In the randomised version the world assigns the wound at the start of the episode before the creature acts, so the pattern describes the creature's disposition. Only the second is a cause.

The effect is small — a few tenths of a point to about one and a half. And all eight creatures came from the same starting seed, so we still cannot separate "the modulator does this" from "these particular upbringings did this". But unlike everything else we measured, it does not flip from pair to pair.

For hunger the same measure shows no consistent modulator effect — two pairs wider, two narrower. Whatever the modulator is doing, it is doing it with injury, not with hunger.

10  How much to trust all this

causes
Anything the world rolled before the creature moved — predator count and eyesight, smells, bush and food counts, starting injury and hunger.
associations
Anything produced during the episode — nearness, injury sustained, nutrition spent. Partly the creature's own doing.
significance
Useless at this scale. With millions of episodes everything is "significant". Everything here is ranked by size of effect, never by significance.
the big one
One training run per condition. This is the limitation that recurs everywhere: it confounded the ten-creature comparison, and it defeated the modulator comparison outright when two matched pairs gave opposite answers. The fix is not more conditions — it is repeats. Three repeats at four settings is worth far more than one repeat at ten, because it measures how much two identical runs differ, which is the number every claim here needs and none of them has.
Hiding is measured as the share of the creature's own chosen steps spent inside a bush; the spawn step is excluded, since it did not choose where it woke. Analysis covers fourteen million replayed episodes across fourteen trained creatures: ten differing in a resting bonus, and four matched modulator pairs. Findings were reviewed adversarially and two were withdrawn after review or replication — a bug in the pain reconstruction, and an over-read of the modulator's effect on perception. Both corrections are recorded in the accompanying reports.
